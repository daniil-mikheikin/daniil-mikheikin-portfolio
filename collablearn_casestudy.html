Case study — CollabLearn

One workspace for the whole group project

CollabLearn is an all-in-one collaboration platform for students and researchers, designed from five interviews, a survey and a secondary study. It answers one question: why does group work still mean a dozen open tabs?

Year2025
ScopeWeb platform, research to hi-fi prototype
RoleProduct design — research, UX, UI
Built inFigma
01Context

Context

University students struggle with fragmented tools and constant app-switching during group and individual projects, which leads to inefficiency and frustration.

CollabLearn was created to solve this by providing a single, user-friendly, all-in-one collaboration workspace.

02Challenge

Challenge

Create an all-in-one collaboration platform that feels familiar and intuitive to students, while introducing new tools and smarter workflows that cut app-switching and measurably improve productivity.

03Solution

Solution

The key was identifying, through interviews and a survey, which tools students actually needed to work together: an integrated chat, flexible data storage, and a task-assignment system.

Each one answers a finding, rather than a guess.

  • Integrated chatConversation lives in the workspace, so nobody leaves the project to ask a question.AnswersIneffective communication tools
  • Flexible data storageAny format, sorted for you by topic and type, and shareable without an upload dance.AnswersInability to store files in different formats
  • Task assignmentRoles and personal tasks attached to real people, visible to everyone.AnswersUnclear role distribution

04Impact

Impact

CollabLearn reduces the fatigue caused by switching between apps, and raises productivity through a built-in chat, AI-powered file sorting, and adaptive workspace modes.

  • Less app-switching fatigueChat, files and tasks stop living in six other places.
  • Communication inside the workMessages appear next to the thing being discussed.
  • Files findable without searchingAuto-sorting by format and topic, at any time.
  • A workspace that adaptsClassic, Creative and Writer, instead of one fixed layout.
05Research

Research

I ran empirical user research with students facing fragmented online tools: five semi-structured interviews with university students working on group projects, exploring app-switching fatigue and inefficient workflows.

Interview excerpt

“If you could magically fix one part of the group-work or research process, what would it be?”

“I think a good solution would be a platform where I could organise the entire process from start to finish.”

All-in-one solution

These insights highlighted the need for a structured tool with integrated features like real-time collaboration and role assignment.

Key findings

Four problems came up in every conversation. They became the brief.

  • 01Ineffective communication toolsConversation happening somewhere the work is not.
  • 02Files that will not storeNo single place that takes every format the group produces.
  • 03Time-consuming citationAcademic writing losing hours to formatting references by hand.
  • 04Unclear role distributionNobody sure who owns what, or whether it is done.
06Secondary research

What the literature already knew

To check what users had told me, I ran a secondary study on the article “The Role of Digital Tools in Enhancing Collaborative Learning in Secondary Education”, which looks at how user-friendly interfaces in tools like Google Classroom and Microsoft Teams change collaboration.

Real-time communication and task management

Instant messaging, file sharing and co-editing reduce coordination barriers, so tasks can be assigned and tracked without a physical meeting.

Positive impacts on engagement

Good design correlates with higher engagement and motivation: students report easier peer interaction and more critical thinking online than face to face.

  • 30%engagement boostwhere the interface is well designed
  • 75%motivation increasereported by students in the same study
  • 68%improve in critical thinkingwhen collaborating through digital tools
  • 82%improve in peer interactioncompared with traditional group dynamics

Source: “The Role of Digital Tools in Enhancing Collaborative Learning in Secondary Education”. These are the study’s figures, not measurements of CollabLearn.

Takeaway

Good design can increase motivation, and real-time chat lowers the barrier to coordinating. Interface design is doing the work here — friendly elements turn a potential demotivator, like an ambiguous open-ended task, into a chance for creative problem solving. The design answer I took from it: a personalised dashboard for every participant.

07Interviews & surveys

What they actually said

The interviews opened with one question: tell me about the last time you were frustrated during a group project. What happened?

  • “It was when I wasn’t able to share a file with my teammate.”

  • “Usually I get frustrated when the outcome of my teammates is not as good as I expected. But I already made peace with that — I either communicate it or, since my team projects are academic, understand that things don’t need to be perfect to be good. If the situation is really bad I like when it’s possible to state what each member did.”

  • “The use of excessive AI from my project partner.”

  • “I’m not familiar with the software, so I feel very anxious.”

Where the problems land

“In group work, where do the biggest problems usually happen?” Four people answered, so this is a direction, not a statistic — and the direction is that the pain is spread evenly rather than concentrated in one place.

  • Task coordination1 of 4
  • Sharing files1 of 4
  • Keeping everyone updated1 of 4
  • The effort people put into the work1 of 4
  • Communication0 of 4
  • Finding or organising research0 of 4

Each circle is one of the four respondents.

Survey notes

Tools participants named: Canva, Miro, Google Forms, WhatsApp, Google and ChatGPT, and Word — used for collecting files, video calls and document collaboration. They also asked for a better face-to-face communication experience.

10Wireframing

Wireframing

Three passes on paper, each one answering a question the last one raised.

01

Iteration 1 — the shape of the room

This early wireframe established the overall layout of the main workspace. The goal was to test where key elements belong — the search bar, the sidebar navigation — and get a logical structure in place from the beginning.

02

Iteration 2 — hierarchy

The second iteration refined the layout, with particular attention to the project title bar. This established a clearer visual hierarchy and more intuitive access to project settings and controls.

03

Finalised layout

The final wireframe became the foundation for the high-fidelity prototype: the search bar repositioned for visibility, a dedicated task-assignment button, active user icons, and a prominent Share button.

The feature that was cut

An AI assistant — the wizard’s hat in the lower-right corner of the early wireframes — was planned to support users. The interviews killed it: trust in AI inside a shared academic workspace was low, and one participant named a partner’s excessive AI use as their worst group-project experience. It was removed from the final design.

12UI design

The interface

The UI takes a calm, minimalist line that keeps people on the task rather than on the chrome. It is meant to feel like a cosy workspace with a bit of structured creativity — an environment that makes learning and collaborating feel worth doing.

01

The main dashboard

Clean, distraction-free navigation that gives users of any experience level instant access to their projects and tools.

02

Classic workspace mode

Classic Mode gives a well-organised set of essential tools that cover most projects. For anything more specialised — advanced creative tools, writing features — a dedicated performance mode is one switch away.

03

Three toolbars, three jobs

The left rail holds citation, shapes, frame and note — including an auto-citation feature that saves real time in academic writing. The bottom bar holds move, docs, draw, text and comment, plus the intelligent file browser. The find bar at the top searches the web without leaving the workspace.

04

Button states

Three states were drawn for every button, so interaction feedback stays consistent wherever a button appears.

13Modes

One workspace, three ways to work

The interviews named the specific tools students need for different kinds of work. I sorted those tools into three groups — Classic, Creative and Writing — and built a workspace mode around each, carrying only the tools that matter for its purpose.

In Writer Mode the interface darkens the background and spotlights the document, which makes a calm, focused environment and removes the distraction of everything else on the canvas.

14The two that matter most

Chat and auto-sorting

These two carry the whole argument. Chat and auto-sorting are built into the workspace: the chat keeps the team connected in real time, and the auto-sorting button organises uploaded files by topic and type the moment they arrive.

01

Real-time chat

The integrated chat solves the app-switching problem and cuts fatigue by keeping every conversation inside the platform, next to the work it is about.

02

Automatic file sorting

AI-powered auto-sorting organises files by format and topic, so they can be found and shared at any time without searching for them, and without leaving for another app.

15Reflection

What the project taught me

This project taught me how a single, adaptive workspace can solve real academic pain points — from constant app-switching to unclear roles and file chaos. By combining real-time chat, AI-powered auto-sorting and flexible modes, the platform creates a calm, focused environment that adapts to different needs.

The biggest lesson was that thoughtful research and user personas lead to genuinely useful features, and to the resolution of real problems.

  • Research earns the featuresEvery tool in the final design traces back to something someone said.
  • Personas are not decorationThree students made the trade-offs concrete when they had to be decided.
  • Listening includes cuttingThe AI assistant was the idea I liked, and the research said no.
